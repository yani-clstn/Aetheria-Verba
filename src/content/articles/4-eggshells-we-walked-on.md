We call it self-preservation, but sometimes it is just fear wearing a noble mask.

When you have been bruised enough times by the sudden shifts in someone’s demeanor, you learn to read the weather of a room before you even step inside. You learn the precise weight of a sigh, the unspoken distance in a brief text, and the quiet tension that signals a storm brewing. 

You start walking on eggshells—careful, deliberate, and constantly braced for the crack beneath your feet.

Eventually, you grow tired of the eggshells. So you start laying bricks instead.

I built my fortress slowly, layer by layer, telling myself it was necessary. I convinced myself that if I loved my solitude enough, if I became entirely self-sufficient, no one could ever reach the places that hurt. I mistook independence for healing and isolation for peace.

I never thought that building my walls so high would make me feel trapped and stuck inside them. 

In my hurry to protect myself from potential pain, I became blind to genuine gentleness. I was so focused on guarding my own heart that I forgot there were still people standing outside the gates, patiently reaching out, trying to love me in the quiet ways I no longer knew how to receive.

It is a lonely realization when you look around the fortress you built to survive, only to find that the very walls meant to keep danger out have kept tenderness from coming in.

Now, I am left sitting in the silence of my own making, wondering if the freedom of solitude was worth the warmth I locked away. Was it really for the better, or did I mistake running away for moving on?
